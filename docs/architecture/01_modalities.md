# 01. The Four Signaling Modalities (Section 2.1)

The IvC framework defines biological coherence not as the outcome of linear codes but as the integration of rhythms across multiple physical modalities. It identifies four core modalities—bioacoustic, bioelectric, biophotonic, and biochemical—through which living systems exchange and synchronize energy. These modalities are woven together through six cellular hubs and embedded in aqueous substrates where proton conduction and phonon dynamics enable cross-modal coupling and energy flow. Evidence shows that each modality is rhythmic and can be entrained. The framework is not metaphorical: it is testable, yielding predictions that experimental disruption or entrainment of one modality will produce measurable coherence shifts in others.

### 2.1.1 Bioacoustic (Mechanical)
This modality encompasses mechanical vibrations across scales. Microtubules and actin filaments resonate in the megahertz to gigahertz range, acting as piezoelectric transducers that convert mechanical stress into electrical signals and back (Sahu et al., 2013; Pokorny, 2004). Membranes propagate nanometer-scale surface waves, while cytoskeletal filaments support traveling vibrations that may couple to enzymatic activity and ion channel gating (Heimburg & Jackson, 2005; Mosgaard & Heimburg, 2013). Phonon-mediated coupling provides a physical basis for coherence; quantized vibrational energy in condensed matter enables ultrafast transfer of energy across proteins, lipids, and water networks (Zhang et al., 2020). Lipid bilayers support gigahertz phonons, while coherent surface acoustic waves in lipid multilayers modulate ion channel function, linking structure to rhythmic signaling (Mosgaard & Heimburg, 2013). Direct evidence that sound waves influence cellular behavior at the most fundamental level comes from a 2025 study by Kyoto University researchers, demonstrating that audible sound frequencies (440 Hz, 14,000 Hz, and white noise) alter gene expression and suppress adipocyte differentiation by up to 15% without physical contact or chemical treatment (Kumamoto et al., 2025).

### 2.1.2 Bioelectric (Electromagnetic)
Bioelectricity extends far beyond neuronal action potentials. Spatial gradients of membrane potential generate long-range patterning fields, governed by ion channel activity and propagated through gap junction networks (Bennett, 2004). These fields guide morphogenesis, as shown in amphibian and planarian regeneration (Levin, 2022; Durant et al., 2023), entrain neural populations during cognition (Doelling & Poeppel, 2024), and synchronize excitable tissues across scales. A central exemplar is the heartbeat: pacemaker cells in the sinoatrial node generate rhythmic voltage oscillations that propagate through cardiac tissue via gap junctions, creating a robust bioelectric rhythm that entrains circulation and couples to respiratory and circadian timing (Casey et al., 2025). This demonstrates how a local oscillatory driver becomes a systemic conductor, binding cellular bioelectric activity to organism-level coherence. Bioelectric activity is bidirectionally linked with other modalities; for example, ultraweak photon emissions can be phase-locked to voltage oscillations (Zangari et al., 2021), and ultrasound stimulation can reset membrane potentials, demonstrating acoustic-electric cross-talk (Tyler, 2017; Yu et al., 2023).

### 2.1.3 Biophotonic (Optical)
Ultraweak photon emissions (UPEs), faint light signals generated by oxidative and metabolic reactions, are a consistent feature of living systems, often originating in mitochondria during metabolic "flashes" (Casey et al., 2025). Evidence indicates that UPEs are not isolated noise but couple with other modalities: mitochondrial redox oscillations align with photonic pulses (Kobayashi et al., 2014), while voltage fluctuations can phase lock with UPE rhythms (Tang & Dai, 2014). Lipid peroxidation at membranes has been identified as another photon source (Pang et al., 2016), linking membrane dynamics such as pearling instabilities to photonic signaling. Modern instrumentation makes this channel tractable for validation; dark box scanning and time-gated tomography with SPAD and SNSPD detectors now allow single-photon resolution in tissues (Popp et al., 2020; Prasad et al., 2023).

### 2.1.4 Biochemical (Molecular)
The foundational rhythms of life are metabolic. Glycolysis, ATP/ADP flux, and redox cycling operate as oscillators that generate reproducible temporal patterns rather than steady states. Classic experiments showed that glycolysis functions as a driven oscillator: external periodic forcing induces phase-locking and reproducible phase-response curves, establishing entrainment as a biochemical principle (Hess & Boiteux, 1971; Wolf et al., 2000). These oscillations provide temporal scaffolding that interfaces with other modalities. Metabolism modulates mitochondrial photon emissions through redox-linked "mitoflashes" (Wang et al., 2008; Casey et al., 2025), and ATP rhythms synchronize vesicle release and trafficking (Rizzuto et al., 2012; Yu et al., 2015). Biochemical rhythms not only sustain energy balance but integrate chemistry and information, creating feedback loops across modalities.
